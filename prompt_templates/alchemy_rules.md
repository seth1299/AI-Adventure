# Alchemy Rules

## What Alchemy Is

Alchemy is often described as “the art of change,” but that phrase is too grand to be useful by itself. A more practical definition is this: alchemy is the craft of extracting, refining, combining, and fixing the active virtues of materials in order to produce a desired effect. Each part of that definition matters.

To extract means to draw out what is useful. A frostmint leaf contains cooling virtue, green bitterness, plant-water, fibrous body, and a faint sleep-heavy aftertone. The cooling virtue may be wanted for a fever draught. The bitterness may be unwanted. The plant-water may weaken the brew. The fibrous body may cloud it. The alchemist must separate the desirable from the undesirable.

To refine means to make a material cleaner, stronger, more stable, or more suitable for use. Raw sun-copper scraped from a ruin may contain dust, old oil, tarnish, and dead enchantment. It cannot be used well in that state. It must be washed, heated, quenched, powdered, sifted, and perhaps fed with a mild acid-spirit before its warming virtue becomes reliable.

To combine means to join materials so that their virtues cooperate rather than quarrel. This is where many beginners fail. Two helpful ingredients may still ruin one another. A swift-stirring nerve tonic may become dangerous if paired with a heart-brightening salt unless a calming binder is added between them. Alchemical ingredients have temperaments. Some lift, some sink, some sharpen, some soften, some awaken, some preserve, some consume.

To fix means to make the result hold its form long enough to be useful. A potion that works for ten breaths after brewing is a curiosity. A potion that remains sound for a month in a waxed bottle is a product. Fixing is the difference between a momentary reaction and a finished alchemical preparation.

The word “virtue” appears often in alchemy. It does not mean moral goodness. It means the active quality or hidden power of a thing. The virtue of emberglass is heat-without-flame. The virtue of grave salt is stillness. The virtue of dawnmoss is renewal. The virtue of silverleaf is clarity. The alchemist’s task is to know these virtues, draw them forth, and arrange them wisely.

## The Stages of Alchemical Work

There are 4 stages of Alchemy: preparation, refinement, synthesis, and Proving.

- Preparation is the stage in which the alchemist identifies, cleans, sorts, measures, and makes ready the raw materials. A surprising amount of alchemy is preparation. The public imagines the alchemist at the dramatic moment when the bottle flashes with green light. The working alchemist knows that this flash is only possible because the moss was dried correctly, the salts were kept from damp air, the flask was clean, and the fire was neither too weak nor too eager.
- Refinement is the stage in which raw materials become reagents. A reagent is a prepared substance suitable for alchemical use. Crushed bluecap mushroom is not yet a reagent. Bluecap tincture, properly strained and clarified, may be. River clay is not a reagent. Purified binding earth, washed until it releases no grit and dried under shade, is. A reagent has been made predictable.
- Synthesis is the stage in which refined reagents are combined to make the desired preparation. A potion, elixir, draught, salve, powder, oil, incense, or crystal charge may all be products of synthesis. This is the stage most people think of as “doing alchemy,” but it rests entirely upon the earlier stages.
- The fourth stage is Proving. To prove an alchemical preparation is to test its quality, stability, strength, and safety by accepted methods. No responsible alchemist gives a fresh brew to a patient or buyer simply because it looks correct. The color, scent, clarity, settling behavior, heat response, charm response, and time stability must all be judged.

## The Common Kinds of Alchemical Products

The common person often uses the word “potion” for every drinkable magical mixture. Alchemists are more precise, though not all regions use the same names.

- A potion is a liquid preparation meant to cause a noticeable effect within a short time. Potions are usually active, direct, and somewhat forceful. A climbing potion, a night-sight potion, and a fever-breaking potion are all examples.
- A draught is generally milder, larger in volume, and intended to support a natural process rather than force a sudden change. A sleep draught invites sleep. A mending draught supports healing. A warming draught helps the body resist cold.
- An elixir is a refined and often concentrated preparation, usually clearer, more stable, and more difficult to make than an ordinary potion. Elixirs are often taken in drops or small sips. They may preserve vitality, sharpen the mind, purify the blood, extend wakefulness, or strengthen resistance to curses.
- A tincture is an extraction of virtue into a carrier, often spirit, oil, vinegar, moonwater, or another receptive medium. Tinctures are often ingredients in later alchemy rather than final products.
- A salve is a preparation worked into fat, wax, resin, or balm and applied to the skin.
- A powder is a dry preparation. Powders may be swallowed, scattered, burned, blown through a reed, added to liquid, or used in circles and seals.
- An incense is a preparation meant to release its virtue through smoke. Incenses often affect mood, memory, spirits, insects, dreams, or the unseen air of a place.
- A philtre is a potion that acts chiefly upon emotion, desire, courage, fear, loyalty, or perception. Philtres are heavily regulated in most civilized kingdoms, since meddling with another person’s will is both dangerous and morally grave.

## The Basic Materials of Alchemy

The alchemist’s materials are usually called reagents once they have been prepared, but before preparation they may be anything: plants, minerals, waters, resins, bones, shells, ashes, metals, fungi, waxes, vapors, scales, clays, salts, seeds, or even light gathered in a crystal vessel.

For the beginner, it is useful to divide materials into several families.

Plant materials are the most approachable. Leaves, roots, bark, flowers, fruit, seeds, sap, thorns, and pollen all carry different virtues. Leaves often hold active and airy qualities, such as swiftness, clarity, scent, and breath. Roots often hold grounding, endurance, memory, sleep, and deep nourishment. Flowers tend toward color, mood, attraction, opening, and delicate sensory effects. Bark is protective and slow. Seeds are potential, fertility, beginnings, and stored force. These are not laws, but they are reliable tendencies.

Mineral materials include stones, crystals, ores, clays, salts, sands, and metals. Minerals are usually more stable than plants but harder to awaken. They lend structure, endurance, sharpness, preservation, and elemental force. Moonstone cools and reflects. Ironthorn ore resists enchantment. Amber salt preserves warmth. Black granite dust grounds excess force. Glass-sand clarifies sight when properly refined.

Animal materials are powerful but require ethical care. Shed feathers, naturally fallen antlers, eggshells, milk, wool, spider silk, and empty cocoons are common lawful materials. Blood, venom, bone, horn, and organs are more restricted and should not be handled by the beginner without supervision. Animal reagents tend to carry motion, instinct, vitality, hunger, protection, voice, and bodily transformation.

Waters are more important than beginners expect. Rainwater, spring water, deep-well water, river water, dew, snowmelt, stormwater, and moonwater are not considered the same in alchemy. Water is not merely a liquid. It is a carrier of condition. Spring water rises and is suited to renewal. Deep-well water remembers stone and silence. Rainwater descends and is suited to cleansing. Stormwater is restless and should be used carefully. Moonwater receives and softens. Sunwater brightens and quickens.

Resins, gums, waxes, and oils are often used to bind, preserve, slow, or carry virtues. A volatile virtue that would vanish in steam may rest comfortably in oil. A protective virtue may be sealed in wax. A fragrant virtue may be preserved in resin and later released through smoke.

Salts are among the alchemist’s most valued helpers. Salt fixes, dries, preserves, sharpens, and separates. Common kitchen salt has small use, but alchemical salts are prepared from ash, mineral deposits, dried seas, cave blooms, and certain plants. White ash salt draws out hidden bitterness. Blue cave salt steadies dream-working. Red hearth salt preserves warmth. Grave salt stills motion and should be used sparingly.

Fires and heats are also treated as materials, though they are not bottled in the usual sense. Candle heat, coal heat, dragon-coal heat, sun heat, fever heat, and blue spirit flame produce different changes. The wise alchemist does not merely ask, “How hot?” but also asks, “What kind of heat?”

## Classification of Alchemecial Products

Alchemical preparations are distinguished by their volume, intensity, and delivery method:

- Potions: Forceful liquid preparations for sudden, noticeable effects.
- Draughts: Milder, larger volumes that support natural processes (e.g., sleep or healing).
- Elixirs: Highly refined, concentrated, and stable; often taken in drops.
- Tinctures: Extractions of virtue into a carrier (spirit, oil, or vinegar); often used as ingredients for more complex works.
- Salves: Worked into fat or wax for topical skin application.
- Philtres: Potions affecting emotion or perception; heavily regulated due to moral implications.
- Incense: Virtues released through smoke to affect mood or the environment.

## The Four Qualities and the Seven Motions

Many schools teach that all alchemical virtues may be understood through four qualities: warm, cool, dry, and moist. These words do not always refer to ordinary temperature or wetness. They describe behavior.

### Qualities and Motions of Reagents

- A warm reagent awakens, expands, stimulates, ripens, or drives outward. A warming draught may not feel hot in the mouth, but it causes the body’s inner fire to rise.
- A cool reagent calms, contracts, preserves, clarifies, or draws inward. A cooling powder may not be cold to the touch, but it calms inflammation or passion.
- A dry reagent separates, hardens, sharpens, fixes, or preserves. A dry essence may be liquid, yet still separate confusion from clarity.
- A moist reagent joins, softens, carries, nourishes, or dissolves. A moist stone may be physically hard, yet help bind quarrelsome reagents together.
  
Alongside the four qualities, practical alchemists speak of seven motions: rising, sinking, opening, closing, quickening, stilling, and turning.

- Rising virtues lift, lighten, vaporize, inspire, or carry effects toward the breath and mind.
- Sinking virtues ground, sedate, stabilize, or carry effects toward the bones and belly.
- Opening virtues unlock, reveal, soften barriers, or begin processes.
- Closing virtues seal, protect, finish, or preserve.
- Quickening virtues speed reactions and bodily responses.
- Stilling virtues slow, calm, quiet, or suspend.
- Turning virtues change direction, reverse a tendency, or transform one quality into another.

When an apprentice understands the four qualities and seven motions, recipes stop looking like arbitrary lists. The apprentice begins to see why a fever draught might combine a cooling flower, a sinking mineral salt, a moist carrier, and a closing wax-seal. The aim is not merely to mix ingredients, but to guide motion.

## Necessary Tools of the Alchemical Workshop

A beginning alchemist does not need a tower, a silver furnace, or a cabinet full of singing crystals. Such things are impressive, but basic alchemy can be learned with modest equipment, provided that the tools are clean, sturdy, and properly used.

- The mortar and pestle are used for crushing, grinding, and blending dry materials. Stone mortars are best for roots, bark, minerals, and salts. Ceramic mortars are good for delicate flowers and powders. Metal mortars are avoided for many preparations because metal can lend its own virtue to the mixture.
- Knives and shears are used to cut plant matter. A good alchemical knife should be easy to clean and used only for workshop materials. Some masters keep different knives for roots, leaves, and animal materials to prevent cross-contamination of virtues.
- Sieves and cloth filters separate fine from coarse and clear from cloudy. A coarse sieve removes stems and grit. A fine silk filter clarifies tinctures. Charcoal cloth removes harshness from certain waters. Never underestimate filtering. A potion can fail simply because too much dead plant-body remains in it.
- Glass vessels are used for observing color, clarity, vapor, and settling. Clear glass is preferred for most work. Green glass shields delicate materials from harsh sunlight. Black glass protects dream and moon preparations. Thick glass is necessary for heating, while thin glass is for storage and observation only.
- The alembic is a distilling device. It allows vapor to rise from a heated material, travel through a cooled passage, and return as liquid. Distillation separates light and volatile virtues from heavier bodies. It is essential for spirits, fragrant waters, clarity essences, and many refined elixirs.
- The retort is a curved vessel used for heating substances and collecting what they release. It is useful when working with minerals, resins, and stubborn plant matter.
- The athanor is the alchemist’s furnace. A true athanor provides steady, patient heat. Ordinary cooking fires are too uneven for delicate alchemy. The beginner may use a small brick furnace, a sand bath, or a regulated lamp, but the principle remains the same: control the heat, and half the battle is won.
- The water bath is used to heat materials gently. A vessel is placed in heated water rather than directly over flame. This prevents scorching and is especially useful for flowers, oils, and delicate extracts.
- The sand bath is similar, but uses heated sand. It provides stronger and drier heat than water. It is suitable for minerals, salts, and thicker mixtures.
- Measuring tools are necessary because alchemy respects proportion. Scales, marked spoons, graduated cups, drop counters, and timing candles all help the alchemist repeat success and avoid repeating failure.
- Stirring rods should be made of glass, bone, polished wood, or other suitable materials. Different rods may be used for different reagent families. A rod used for grave salt should not be used casually in a vitality cordial.
- Sealing materials include wax, cork, resin, glass stoppers, clay plugs, and binding thread. A finished potion that is not sealed properly will change, weaken, sour, or become unsafe.
- Labels and records are as important as any furnace. Every vessel should be labeled with contents, date, source, and stage of preparation. Every process should be recorded. Many apprentices think they will remember what they did. They will not. The workshop notebook is the alchemist’s second mind.

### Gathering Raw Materials

Good alchemy begins before the workshop. It begins at the place of gathering.

- The first rule of gathering is correct identification. Many plants, stones, and fungi resemble one another. The useful dawnmoss has a golden underside and a scent like warm hay. False dawnmoss has a yellow underside and no scent. In a basket they appear nearly identical, but false dawnmoss causes a mending draught to clot. The careful gatherer checks shape, color, scent, habitat, season, and behavior.
- The second rule is proper timing. Some materials are strongest at dawn, others at dusk. Some roots should be dug in late autumn, after the plant has withdrawn its virtue below ground. Some flowers must be picked at first opening. Some crystals should be collected after moonrise, when their reflective virtue is awake. Timing is not superstition. It is observation repeated across generations.
- The third rule is cleanliness. Dirt, insects, mold, old leaves, rust, and foreign dust all change results. Raw materials need not be spotless in the field, but they should be gathered into clean containers and sorted promptly.
- The fourth rule is restraint. Never strip a whole patch of plants. Never take every egg, every crystal, or every resin bead. Alchemy depends on the world remaining alive. Wasteful gathering is both foolish and dishonorable.
- The fifth rule is separation. Do not throw all materials into one sack. Strong-smelling herbs can taint delicate flowers. Damp fungi can spoil dry bark. Salts can draw moisture from leaves. Animal materials should be wrapped separately. A gathering basket with divided compartments is better than a heroic heap.

### Preparing Raw Materials

Once materials reach the workshop, they must be prepared. Preparation usually begins with inspection. Anything moldy, rotten, insect-eaten, spiritually dulled, or wrongly gathered should be discarded or set aside for noncritical use. A flawed reagent may be harmless in incense but disastrous in medicine.

- Washing is used for roots, stones, shells, and some leaves. Washing should be gentle. Many delicate materials lose virtue in water. Flowers are often brushed rather than washed. Mushrooms may be wiped with damp cloth. Salts are usually not washed unless the intent is purification through dissolving and recrystallizing.
- Drying prevents decay and concentrates certain virtues. Shade drying is slow and gentle, preserving color and subtle qualities. Sun drying is faster and adds brightness but may drive off moon or dream virtues. Smoke drying adds preservation and sometimes protective qualities. Freeze drying, practiced in northern schools, preserves clarity and is useful for frost herbs.
- Cutting and bruising open the body of a material. A bruised leaf releases its scent. A cut root exposes its inner channels. Too much crushing too soon can waste volatile virtues. The wise alchemist asks whether the material should be opened now, later, or not at all.
- Sorting by part is necessary. Leaf, stem, root, flower, seed, and bark should not be treated as one substance. A whole plant may contain opposing virtues. For example, lanternweed leaves brighten vision, while its root induces mild confusion. A careless apprentice who dries the whole plant together may produce a potion that helps the drinker see twice as clearly and understand half as much.
- Storing prepared materials requires attention. Dry herbs should be kept in breathable paper or cloth until fully dry, then sealed away from damp. Oils should be kept in dark glass. Salts should be kept dry. Moon reagents should be protected from direct sun. Fire reagents should not be stored beside sleep reagents or volatile spirits. The workshop shelf should be arranged by logic, not convenience alone.

### Refinement: Turning Materials into Reagents

Refinement is the process by which a raw material becomes reliable. There are many methods of refinement, but the beginner should understand the most common: grinding, infusion, decoction, maceration, distillation, calcination, dissolution, crystallization, fermentation, clarification, and fixation.

- Grinding reduces a material to smaller parts. This increases surface area, allowing its virtue to be extracted more easily. However, grinding also exposes the material to air and can cause loss of scent, moisture, or subtle force. Soft leaves should be crumbled, not pulverized. Hard roots may be powdered. Minerals often require long grinding and sifting. The finest powder is not always the best. Sometimes a coarse powder extracts more cleanly because it does not clog filters.
- Infusion means steeping a material in hot or cold liquid to draw out its virtue. Tea is a household infusion. In alchemy, infusions may use water, moonwater, oil, vinegar, spirit, or other carriers. Hot infusion is quick and strong but may damage delicate virtues. Cold infusion is slow and gentle. A silverleaf clarity infusion is commonly made cold, because boiling makes it harsh and overly sharp.
- Decoction means simmering tougher materials, such as roots, bark, seeds, and some minerals, to draw out virtues that will not yield to simple steeping. Decoctions are stronger and heavier than infusions. They often need careful filtering and settling.
- Maceration means soaking a material for a long period until the carrier penetrates and softens it. This is useful for resins, dried fruits, thick roots, and certain animal materials. Maceration may last days or weeks. The vessel must be sealed and shaken or turned at intervals.
- Distillation separates volatile virtues by heat and condensation. When a fragrant herb is distilled, its light essence rises in vapor and returns as a clearer, subtler liquid. Distillation is used when the desired quality is airy, fragrant, clarifying, or easily spoiled by heavy plant matter. Poor distillation produces flat, lifeless water. Good distillation captures the living breath of a material.
- Calcination means reducing a material by controlled burning or intense heating until ash, lime, or mineral body remains. This is not ordinary burning. The aim is not destruction but the removal of excess moisture, oil, and softness. Calcined bone, shell, herb ash, and mineral powders are used in salts, fixatives, and grounding reagents. Calcination is dangerous when done carelessly, and no apprentice should perform it without proper ventilation and instruction.
- Dissolution means dissolving a material into a liquid so that its parts can be separated or transformed. Some salts must be dissolved, filtered, and dried again to remove impurities. Some minerals release their virtue only after being softened by sour wine, spirit vinegar, or a prepared alchemical solvent.
- Crystallization is the controlled return of a dissolved material into solid form. As the liquid evaporates or cools, purified crystals form. The shape, clarity, and color of the crystals reveal their quality. Jagged, cloudy crystals suggest haste or contamination. Clean, even crystals suggest orderly refinement.
- Fermentation is the controlled transformation of a material by time, warmth, and living change. Fermented reagents can develop depth, potency, and complexity. Honey ferments, fruit ferments, and grain spirits are common carriers in many traditions. Fermentation is useful but unruly. A ferment must be watched, smelled, vented, and recorded. If it turns foul, it is not “mysteriously powerful.” It is spoiled.
- Clarification removes cloudiness, sediment, harshness, and unwanted body. It may involve settling, filtering, skimming, egg-shell binding, charcoal cloth, silver stirring, or moon-resting. A clear potion is not always better than a cloudy one, but clarity is often a sign of stability.
- Fixation makes a reagent hold its virtue. This may be done with salt, resin, wax, honey, alcohol, cooling, sealing, or a stabilizing charm. A swift essence without fixation may fade in a day. The same essence properly fixed may last a season.

### The Principle of Carriers

A carrier is the medium that receives and holds a virtue. Water, oil, alcohol, vinegar, honey, wax, fat, smoke, salt, and crystal can all serve as carriers.

- Water carries quickly and naturally into the body. It is suitable for common draughts and gentle potions, but it spoils easily and does not hold all virtues well.
- Alcoholic spirit carries volatile, warming, and penetrating virtues. It preserves well and acts quickly, but it can be too forceful for delicate preparations.
- Oil carries warmth, soothing, protection, fragrance, and skinward virtues. Oil preparations are often salves, rubs, or slow internal medicines in traditions where such use is permitted.
- Vinegar carries sharp, cleansing, separating, and awakening virtues. It is useful for extracting minerals and cutting through heaviness.
- Honey carries nourishment, preservation, sweetness, and harmonizing qualities. It is often used in restorative draughts and child-safe preparations, though dosage and constitution still matter.
- Wax and resin carry sealing, protection, and slow release. They are used in salves, incense, tablets, and preserved charms.
- Salt carries dryness, preservation, separation, and fixation. It is often used in tiny amounts to stabilize.
- Crystal carries pattern. A crystal may hold a virtue in a structured form, especially for light, sound, memory, and warding work. Crystal alchemy is beautiful but unforgiving; flaws in the crystal can distort the result.
- Choosing the correct carrier is one of the most important decisions in synthesis. A calming flower placed in stormwater may become agitating. A brightening herb placed in heavy oil may become dull. A protective resin dissolved in spirit may act too sharply, while the same resin warmed into wax may become steady and useful.

### Synthesis: Making the Potion

Once the reagents are refined, synthesis begins. Though recipes differ, most potion-making follows a common order: establish the base, introduce the primary virtue, balance with secondary virtues, bind the mixture, awaken it, clarify it, prove it, and bottle it.

The base is the main body of the potion. It may be water, spirit, oil, syrup, broth, or another carrier. The base determines how the potion moves. A water-based potion acts more quickly and passes cleanly. A syrup-based draught is slower, gentler, and nourishing. A spirit-based elixir is penetrating and long-lasting. The base should be chosen before the other reagents are combined.

The primary virtue is the main intended effect. In a night-sight potion, the primary virtue may come from silverleaf, owlshade berry, or glass-sand distillate. In a courage draught, it may come from lionroot, sunpepper, or ember honey. The primary virtue should be introduced with care, often in stages, so it does not overwhelm the base.

Secondary virtues support, guide, or soften the primary virtue. A night-sight potion may need a calming reagent to prevent eye strain, a moistening reagent to prevent dryness, and a grounding reagent to keep the drinker from becoming dizzy. A courage draught may need a cooling reagent to prevent recklessness. Many failed potions are not weak; they are unbalanced.

The binder helps the ingredients cooperate. Binders may be honey, salt, gum, egg-shell lime, moon resin, powdered pearl, or a harmonizing tincture. The binder is often added when the mixture first shows signs of separation, harsh scent, sudden color streaks, or unstable foam.

Awakening is the moment when heat, cold, stirring, spoken charm, light exposure, or another stimulus brings the mixture into active unity. In common alchemy, awakening may be as simple as warming the vessel until the color turns clear. In more subtle work, it may require stirring at a particular rhythm, exposing the vessel to dawn light, or cooling it over a bowl of snow.

Clarification follows awakening. The potion is allowed to settle, then filtered, skimmed, or decanted. This removes spent matter. A potion that contains too much spent reagent may continue reacting in the bottle, becoming stronger, weaker, sour, or dangerous.

Proving tests whether the potion is what it claims to be. The alchemist examines color, scent, texture, sediment, response to warmth, response to a charm-thread, and sometimes a tiny test on a proving stone. A healing draught might be touched to a cut reed to see whether it closes cleanly. A night-sight potion might be dropped on pale glass to see whether it gathers dim light. These tests do not replace caution, but they reveal obvious faults.

Bottling is the final act of synthesis. The bottle must be clean, dry or properly wetted depending on the potion, and suitable to the preparation. Some potions require dark glass. Some require a wax seal. Some must be stored cool. Some must be shaken before use, while others must never be shaken. The label should state the name, purpose, date, strength, dose, warnings, and maker.

### An Example: A Simple Warming Draught

To make the process less abstract, consider a simple fictional preparation called a hearth-warming draught. It is not meant to set the blood on fire or grant heroic endurance against blizzards. It is a modest draught used by travelers, night watchmen, and chilled workers to restore comfortable warmth.

The primary virtue is gentle warmth. This might come from emberseed, a small red seed gathered from hearthbush after the first frost. Emberseed is too sharp when raw, so it must be softened.

The base is spring water, because the draught should be drinkable and quick. A spoon of amber honey is chosen as a binder and comforter. A pinch of red hearth salt is chosen to fix the warmth. A strip of winterbark is added as a grounding secondary virtue, so the warmth settles into the body rather than rushing to the face.

First, the emberseed is inspected. Cracked black seeds are discarded. The good seeds are warmed gently in a dry pan until they release a breadlike scent. They are not scorched. Then they are crushed coarsely.

The winterbark is simmered in spring water to make a mild decoction. This draws out its steadying virtue. The liquid is strained. While still warm, the crushed emberseed is added and allowed to infuse. The vessel is covered, because the warming virtue rises with steam if neglected.

After steeping, the liquid is strained again. Amber honey is stirred in slowly until the draught turns clear gold. The red hearth salt is added last, no more than a pinch, and the draught is stirred clockwise until the surface stops trembling. It is then rested near, not over, a small flame.

The finished draught should smell faintly of toast, resin, and honey. It should not sting the nose. Its color should be amber, not red. Redness indicates too much emberseed or too much heat. A good hearth-warming draught gives gradual warmth in the hands and chest. A poor one causes sweating, thirst, or irritability.

This example shows the basic logic of alchemy. The warming ingredient provides the effect, but it is prepared to reduce harshness. The bark guides the warmth. The honey harmonizes. The salt fixes. The heat awakens. The filtering removes spent matter. Nothing is included merely because it sounds impressive.

### Conclusion

Alchemy is a practical art of transformation. It begins with the belief that materials possess hidden virtues, but it does not end in belief.

It demands observation, preparation, patience, measurement, cleanliness, ethical judgment, and respect for the living world.
The process may be summarized plainly. First, know the material. Then gather it properly. Clean and prepare it. Refine it into a reliable reagent.

Choose a suitable carrier. Combine the reagents in an order that respects their qualities and motions. Bind them so they cooperate. Awaken the mixture through heat, cold, light, stirring, or time. Clarify it. Test it. Bottle it. Label it. Watch how it behaves. Record everything.

To the outsider, the final potion may look like magic in a bottle. To the alchemist, it is the visible result of many small acts done correctly. The wonder is real, but it is not careless. It is disciplined wonder. Alchemy takes much effort and high knowledge, so the cost of its final products is high.
